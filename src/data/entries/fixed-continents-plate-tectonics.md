By the early twentieth century, most geologists still treated continents and ocean basins as essentially fixed in place.

Alfred Wegener challenged that view in 1912. Continents on opposite sides of the Atlantic seemed to fit together, and matching fossils and rock formations suggested that they had once been joined. Many geologists remained unconvinced because Wegener could not explain how continents could move through the ocean floor.

The breakthrough came from studying the oceans themselves. Sonar mapping revealed long mountain chains on the seafloor, now called mid-ocean ridges. New crust forms along these ridges and moves outward on both sides.

Magnetic measurements provided a striking record of that movement. As new seafloor cooled, its rocks recorded the direction of Earth's magnetic field. Because the field has reversed many times, the ocean floor developed matching bands of opposite magnetic direction on either side of the ridges. Older seafloor was also found progressively farther from them.

Earthquakes and volcanoes supplied another piece of the picture by tracing the boundaries between moving plates. By the 1960s, plate tectonics brought the evidence together: continents move because they are parts of larger plates, not because they plough independently through a stationary ocean floor.

Plate motion can now be measured directly. Questions remain about how plate tectonics began and exactly what drives it, but the older fixed-Earth model is no longer viable.
