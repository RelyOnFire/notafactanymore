Male-biased body size is conspicuous in many familiar mammals. Male elephant seals, deer, lions, and many primates are larger than females, and the pattern has often been generalized to mammals as a whole.

A 2024 analysis tested whether larger males actually occur in most species. The researchers assembled sex-specific body-mass data for 429 wild mammal species and weighted mammalian families by their overall species richness. That step matters because heavily studied groups such as primates, carnivores, and hoofed mammals contain many strongly male-biased species, while rodents and bats make up a much larger share of mammalian diversity.

The resulting estimate was 45.1% male-larger species, 38.7% approximately monomorphic species, and 16.2% female-larger species. Larger males remained the most common single category, but not a majority.

The dataset covers only about 5% of living mammal species, so those percentages are not a final census. The correction is narrower: the familiar statement that males are larger in *most* mammal species is not supported by the best taxonomically balanced estimate currently available.
