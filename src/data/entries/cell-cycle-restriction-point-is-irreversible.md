The mammalian cell cycle has long been described as containing a restriction point in G1. Before that point, loss of growth signals can return a cell to quiescence. After it, the cell was thought to be irreversibly committed to DNA replication and division.

The model originated with Arthur Pardee's 1974 work and later acquired a molecular explanation involving CDK4/6, E2F, and CDK2. Once CDK2 activity became self-sustaining, the cell was expected to continue through S phase and mitosis even if external mitogens disappeared.

Single-cell experiments published in 2023 showed that this commitment is not absolute. When mitogen or CDK4/6 signalling was removed during S or G2, CDK2 activity could still decline and cells could exit the cycle before mitosis. Delaying mitosis made this reversibility especially clear.

The restriction-point phenomenon remains useful: under ordinary conditions, many cells that pass late G1 will still complete division after growth-factor withdrawal. The overturned claim is that the point represents an irreversible molecular switch. Proliferation remains dependent on signalling later into interphase than the classical model implied.
