Measuring the rotation of an ice giant is harder than timing a solid surface. Uranus’s visible atmosphere moves at different speeds with latitude, so Voyager 2 used radio and magnetic measurements during its 1986 flyby to define a deep rotation period of about 17.24 hours. That value became the basis of the planet’s standard longitude system.

The measurement was close, but its uncertainty accumulated with time. Decades later, the Voyager-based longitude system had drifted enough that the predicted orientation of Uranus’s magnetic field no longer lined up reliably with new observations.

Hubble supplied a much longer baseline by tracking ultraviolet aurorae associated with the magnetic poles from 2011 to 2022. The resulting period, 17.247864 hours, is only about 28 seconds longer than the Voyager value. Its much higher precision is the important change: the revised period supports a longitude system that remains coherent across decades of observations.
