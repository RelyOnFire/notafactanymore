For much of modern medicine, the healthy lower respiratory tract was described as sterile. Standard cultures from healthy lungs usually failed to grow organisms, while bacteria recovered from respiratory samples could be dismissed as contamination from the mouth or as evidence of infection.

The assumption fit the tools. Clinical culture methods were designed primarily to detect pathogens that grow readily under laboratory conditions, not to characterize sparse, complex microbial communities.

Culture-independent techniques changed the question. Sequencing bacterial genetic material from carefully obtained lower-airway samples repeatedly detected microbial populations in people without pneumonia or other overt infection. Improved sampling and analytical controls helped distinguish genuine low-biomass lung communities from contamination.

The modern lung microbiome is not simply a miniature version of the gut. Microbial density is far lower, and the community is continually shaped by inhalation, tiny amounts of aspiration from the upper airway, immune clearance and local growth conditions.

Finding microbial DNA also does not mean every detected organism is actively colonizing the lung or causing disease. The important correction is to the old sterile-versus-infected binary: healthy lower airways can contain microbial communities without being infected.
