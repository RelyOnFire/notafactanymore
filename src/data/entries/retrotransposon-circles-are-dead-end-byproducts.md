LTR retrotransposons copy themselves through an RNA intermediate. After RNA is copied back into DNA, the familiar route ends with a new DNA copy inserted into the host genome.

Circular copies found outside the chromosomes were usually treated as side products of that process. Earlier work described such circles as dead ends: DNA that had failed to follow the productive route into the genome and had instead been joined into a ring.

A 2023 study followed newly replicated retrotransposon DNA with long-read sequencing. In the experimental system, only a minority of detected products became new genome insertions. Roughly 90 percent appeared as extrachromosomal circular DNA.

Genetic experiments then showed that formation of the dominant circles depended on alternative end joining, a host DNA-repair pathway. The retrotransposons were recruiting that machinery during synthesis of their second DNA strand. The circles were therefore not simply debris left after an unsuccessful integration attempt.

Their later biological roles are still being worked out. The study did not prove that every retrotransposon circle is useful or that every circle can seed a new insertion. The important change is mechanistic: circularization itself can be a central, actively generated step in retrotransposon replication.
