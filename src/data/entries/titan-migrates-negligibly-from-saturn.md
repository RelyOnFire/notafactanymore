Tides raised on Saturn transfer angular momentum to moons outside the planet’s synchronous orbit, causing them to move gradually outward. Standard tidal models made that migration fall steeply with distance. For a remote moon such as Titan, the expected motion was therefore very small over much of Solar System history.

Cassini provided enough precision to measure the change directly. Two independent methods, long-term astrometry and radiometric tracking of the spacecraft, found Titan migrating outward at a rate more than 100 times larger than many conventional estimates. The observations favored resonance locking, in which evolving oscillation modes inside Saturn can keep a moon strongly coupled to the planet’s tides.

The faster rate changes reconstructions of the Saturn system. Titan and the other moons may have moved much farther since formation than older models allowed, affecting the timing of resonances and episodes of tidal heating. The broader lesson is that distance alone does not determine how quickly a moon can migrate around an evolving giant planet.
