Few discoveries made mutation feel as physical as Hermann J. Muller's fruit-fly experiments. In 1927 he showed that X-rays could drive up the number of inherited mutations; the work earned him the 1946 Nobel Prize. The implication was hard to ignore. If an artificial beam could alter heredity, then the weak radiation always arriving from cosmic rays, radioactive rocks, building materials, and isotopes inside living tissue looked like nature's own mutation generator. Muller wrote in 1941 that natural radioactivity might appreciably influence mutation frequency and could matter to evolution. In 1956, amid intense concern over nuclear fallout, the U.S. National Academy of Sciences' BEAR Genetics Committee concluded that background radiation supplied enough mutations for evolutionary purposes.

Radiation really does mutate DNA. The surprise is how little of it reaches a tiny cell at ordinary background levels. Modeling for an *E. coli* evolution experiment estimated only about 6 × 10^-5 radiation-track interactions per cell per day at the surface laboratory—roughly one hit for every 17,000 cells. The expected daily point-mutation rate was about 100 times higher. Meanwhile, cells generate mutations through DNA-copying errors, spontaneous chemical changes, reactive molecules, transcription, recombination, and imperfect repair. Once mutation could be counted rather than merely induced, background radiation looked much less capable of supplying the bulk of evolutionary variation.

The cleanest test took evolution underground. Researchers grew 24 *E. coli* populations in a Lenski-style serial-transfer experiment, split between a surface laboratory in Clermont-Ferrand and the Modane Underground Laboratory beneath 1,700 m of rock in the French Alps. The dose rate fell from 214 nGy per hour at the surface to 26 nGy per hour underground—a 6.8-fold reduction. Even there the background could not be driven to zero: about 99% of the remaining dose came from potassium-40 in the growth medium. If natural radiation supplied an important share of beneficial mutations, the underground populations should have adapted more slowly. After 500 generations, mean relative fitness had risen by about 12% at the surface and 9% underground; the difference was not statistically significant. Direct competitions likewise found no meaningful fitness disadvantage in the underground lines.

As the mutation-supply idea weakened, a new puzzle appeared. Take almost all background radiation away and some microbes do notice: ultra-low-radiation experiments have reported altered growth, stress responses, and gene expression. In *Shewanella oneidensis*, deprivation of background radiation triggered a broad transcriptional stress response. Cells may be physiologically tuned to a world that has always been faintly radioactive. In the 2019 evolution experiment, however, removing most of that radiation did not deprive *E. coli* of the variation it needed to keep adapting.
