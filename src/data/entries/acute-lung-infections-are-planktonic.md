A standard microbiology distinction linked acute infection with fast-growing, free-swimming bacteria and chronic infection with slow-growing bacteria organized into biofilms. The model was especially influential in lung disease, where chronic infections such as those in cystic fibrosis are well known for dense bacterial aggregates.

The acute side of the comparison had been studied much less directly.

In 2022, researchers used advanced imaging on 43 fresh sputum samples from people with acute and chronic lung infections. Biofilms dominated both groups. Planktonic bacteria were present, but acute pneumonia samples were not simply populations of freely suspended cells.

The major difference was growth rate. Bacteria in acute infections were growing faster, while bacteria in chronic infections grew more slowly, even though both commonly formed biofilm aggregates.

The result does not mean every acute bacterial infection in the body is biofilm-dominated. The evidence came from human lung infections. Within that setting, however, the simple equation of acute with planktonic and chronic with biofilm no longer holds.
