By the middle of the 20th century, heredity and viral replication were increasingly understood in terms of nucleic acids. An infectious agent that could reproduce its defining properties without carrying DNA or RNA therefore seemed difficult to reconcile with molecular biology.

Scrapie made the problem stubborn. The transmissible agent behaved unlike familiar viruses and resisted repeated attempts to identify a conventional genome. Stanley Prusiner argued in 1982 that the agent was a "proteinaceous infectious particle," later shortened to prion. The proposal was controversial precisely because it seemed to require biological information to propagate without its own nucleic-acid sequence.

The solution was a different kind of templating. A misfolded prion protein can induce normally folded versions of the same host protein to adopt the abnormal conformation, allowing the pathological state to multiply and spread. Different conformations can also help account for prion strain behavior. The host still supplies the gene encoding normal prion protein; what the infectious particle lacks is a separate DNA or RNA genome of its own.
