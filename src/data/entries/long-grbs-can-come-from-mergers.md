Gamma-ray bursts were commonly divided by duration. Long bursts, lasting more than about two seconds, were associated with the collapse of massive stars. Short bursts were associated with mergers involving neutron stars or black holes.

That division worked well enough to become a standard shorthand, but it was never perfect. Some bursts already had mixed properties that did not fit neatly into either class.

GRB 211211A made the problem much harder to ignore. Detected in 2021 and analyzed in detail in 2022, the burst lasted about 50 seconds. By duration it looked long. Its afterglow, location and lack of a bright supernova did not fit an ordinary collapsar, and its optical and infrared counterpart was consistent with a kilonova produced by a compact-object merger.

The result was reinforced in 2023 by GRB 230307A. It lasted about 35 seconds, yet JWST observations identified a kilonova associated with the event.

Most classical long gamma-ray bursts are still linked to massive-star collapse. The correction is narrower: duration is not a reliable one-to-one label for the underlying explosion. A merger can produce a burst that lasts tens of seconds.
