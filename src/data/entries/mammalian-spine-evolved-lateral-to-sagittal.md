A long-standing model of mammalian evolution described a straightforward change in spinal movement. Early synapsids, with their sprawling limbs, were assumed to bend their backs from side to side like living lizards. Mammals later evolved the more familiar up-and-down, or sagittal, flexion used in running.

The model relied heavily on living reptiles as functional analogues for extinct synapsids. In 2021, researchers instead compared the three-dimensional vertebral shapes of fossil synapsids with those of modern reptiles and mammals.

The fossils did not fall on a simple reptile-to-mammal continuum. Early synapsid vertebrae occupied a distinct functional region and appear to have been relatively stiff rather than highly specialized for lateral bending. Later mammalian evolution added greater sagittal flexion in some regions, more axial twisting in others, and stronger regional specialization along the spine.

Mammals really did evolve important new forms of spinal movement, so the old model was not wrong about the endpoints. What changed was the path between them. Early mammal-line animals were not simply lizard-like lateral benders waiting to acquire sagittal motion; their backs had a distinct functional organization of their own.
