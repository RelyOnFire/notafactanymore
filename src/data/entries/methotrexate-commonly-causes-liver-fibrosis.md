Methotrexate has been used for decades to treat diseases including rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis. Because liver abnormalities were observed during long-term treatment, cumulative exposure came to be regarded as an important cause of chronic liver fibrosis.

That concern shaped practice. Patients could face repeated blood testing and, historically, liver biopsies as their lifetime methotrexate dose increased.

The problem was separating the drug from the people receiving it. Conditions such as obesity, diabetes and fatty liver disease are common in the same patient populations and can themselves produce fibrosis.

A large prospective study published in 2023 examined 999 patients with rheumatoid arthritis or psoriasis using two validated non-invasive measures of liver fibrosis. Higher age and body-mass index were associated with abnormal results. Cumulative methotrexate dose and treatment duration were not associated with increased liver stiffness.

This does not make methotrexate harmless to the liver. Acute liver injury can occur, and people with underlying fatty liver disease may face additional risk. What has changed is the older cumulative-toxicity model in which long-term methotrexate itself was expected to commonly drive progressive fibrosis. Modern evidence places much more weight on the patient's underlying metabolic liver risk.
