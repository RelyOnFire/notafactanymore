A clear piece of rock crystal could look like something brought down from a glacier. That resemblance carried explanatory weight. In his first-century Natural History, Pliny connected crystal with moisture hardened by extreme cold. Its association with snowy mountain regions appeared to support the idea.

The claim survived in learned natural history, although it never went unchallenged. Thomas Browne devoted a chapter of Pseudodoxia Epidemica to it in 1646. He named earlier critics, including the mineralogist Georgius Agricola, and asked readers to compare the substances rather than rely on their resemblance.

Several observations pulled the explanation apart. Ice floated in water, whereas rock crystal sank. They responded differently to warmth. Reports of crystal from hot countries and from mineral veins also challenged the proposed connection with lasting snow. The disagreement concerned which observations an explanation could accommodate, as well as the standing of inherited authorities.

Browne's own account of how crystal formed still invoked ideas about petrification that belong to his period. Rejecting permanent ice did not give him the modern chemistry of quartz. The 1646 date marks a published intervention in a longer dispute; it is not the year everyone changed their mind. For that reason, this entry is excluded from belief-lifespan comparisons.

Modern mineralogy identifies rock crystal as clear quartz, SiO₂. Its resemblance to frozen water remains visible, but resemblance alone cannot establish what a material is made of.
