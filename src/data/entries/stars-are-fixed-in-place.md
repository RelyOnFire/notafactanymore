“Fixed stars” originally meant something more observationally specific than “stars are nailed permanently to the universe.” The planets visibly wandered against the stellar background; the stars, by contrast, kept essentially the same patterns and separations over ordinary human timescales. Ptolemy explicitly explained the term this way in the *Almagest*: they were called fixed because they appeared to preserve their figures and relative distances.

That apparent stability made sense within ancient and medieval stellar astronomy. Ptolemy's second-century *Almagest* compiled a sophisticated mathematical astronomy and star catalogue and remained a basic astronomical text for more than a thousand years. The constellations really are stable enough that ancient catalogues remained useful reference points.

Ironically, those same old catalogues eventually helped expose the limitation. Edmond Halley compared eighteenth-century stellar positions with ancient and early-modern measurements and argued in 1717/1718 that several bright stars had moved. Modern reanalysis shows that Halley's evidence was not statistically secure. Jacques Cassini's 1738 work on Arcturus provides a cleaner first significant proper-motion detection.

Modern astrometry turns this tiny proper motion into a vast map of stellar movement. Stars move through the Milky Way at different velocities and distances; their constellations only look fixed because stellar distances are enormous and human lifetimes are short.
