Mitochondria and chloroplasts look like components of complex cells, so it was natural to treat them as structures that had evolved entirely inside those cells. Proposals that they descended from formerly free-living microbes existed early, but for decades most cell biologists regarded endosymbiosis as speculative.

The organelles themselves kept supplying clues. They divide in a bacteria-like manner, possess their own DNA, and contain ribosomes that differ from those in the surrounding eukaryotic cytoplasm. Their membranes and biochemical machinery also preserve features with clear bacterial parallels.

Lynn Margulis revived and synthesized the endosymbiotic case in the 1960s, but the strongest confirmation came from molecular data. Organelle genes could be compared directly with bacterial genes, placing mitochondria within bacterial ancestry and chloroplasts within cyanobacterial ancestry.

The modern picture is therefore historical as well as cellular: an ancestral host incorporated bacteria that became permanent partners and, over evolutionary time, transferred many genes to the host nucleus.

The core endosymbiotic origin is well established. Details about the host cell and the earliest stages remain active research, and not every proposed endosymbiotic explanation for other organelles has received comparable support.
