Woodpeckers strike trees thousands of times without suffering the sort of head injuries that similar accelerations might cause in larger animals. A common explanation was that their beak, skull and hyoid apparatus formed a shock-absorbing system that cushioned the brain.

That idea influenced both biology and engineering. Woodpecker anatomy was even used as inspiration for helmets and impact-resistant materials.

In 2022, researchers measured the motion of the beak and braincase during real pecking in three woodpecker species. If the skull were absorbing much of the shock, the braincase should decelerate less sharply than the beak. It did not. The head behaved very stiffly, transmitting the motion rather than cushioning it.

That stiffness also makes functional sense. A hammer works better when little of its kinetic energy is absorbed by the hammer itself.

The birds still avoid concussion, but not because the skull acts like a crumple zone. Models indicated that their small brains and cranial geometry keep the resulting pressures below estimated injury thresholds. Other anatomical features may still matter, but the central shock-absorber explanation did not survive direct measurement.
