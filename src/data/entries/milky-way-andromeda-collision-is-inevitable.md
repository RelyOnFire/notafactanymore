For more than a century, astronomers have known that the Andromeda galaxy is approaching the Milky Way. The missing part of the motion was sideways. A galaxy can be coming toward us while still passing safely to one side, so predicting a collision required measuring an extraordinarily small drift across the sky.

In 2012, a Hubble Space Telescope team reported the direct measurements needed to do that. Andromeda's sideways motion appeared so small that NASA announced the result in unusually definite language: astronomers could now predict the coming collision "with certainty." Simulations put the first major encounter roughly four billion years in the future and the eventual merger a couple of billion years later.

The prediction was reasonable given the measurements and modelling available at the time. It also became one of astronomy's most familiar pictures of the far future: Andromeda swelling across the night sky, the two spiral galaxies passing through each other, and their stars eventually settling into a merged remnant.

The uncertainty turned out to be more important than that picture suggested.

By 2025, astronomers had additional Hubble observations, Gaia astrometry and better estimates for the masses and motions of the Local Group. They also modelled the gravitational effects of two other substantial galaxies: M33, a satellite of Andromeda, and the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite of the Milky Way. M33 tends to make a merger more likely. The Large Magellanic Cloud pulls the Milky Way in a direction that makes it less likely.

The new study did something else that mattered: instead of evolving only one set of best-estimate values into the future, it sampled the observational uncertainties across many possible Local Groups. Those slightly different starting conditions produced dramatically different outcomes.

In the full four-galaxy model, only about half of the simulated futures produced a Milky Way–Andromeda merger within 10 billion years. Some encounters were close enough for dynamical friction to bring the galaxies back together later; in many others they passed too far apart for a merger on that timescale.

Andromeda is still approaching us, and a future merger remains entirely possible. What changed is the certainty. The iconic collision is now one possible future of the Local Group rather than an unavoidable appointment already written into the sky.
