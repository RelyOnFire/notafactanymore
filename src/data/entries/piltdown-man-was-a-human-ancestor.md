Piltdown Man seemed to supply exactly the kind of fossil some early twentieth-century researchers expected. The remains combined a relatively human-like braincase with an ape-like jaw, apparently suggesting that a large brain had evolved early in human ancestry.

Announced in 1912 from Piltdown, England, the specimen gained institutional credibility and influenced reconstructions of human evolution for decades.

Not everyone was convinced. The jaw and skull looked anatomically mismatched, and later discoveries in Africa and Asia increasingly made Piltdown an awkward outlier.

New tests finally dismantled it. Fluorine dating showed that the pieces were not equally ancient, while microscopic examination revealed filed teeth and artificial staining. The “fossil” had been assembled from human skull fragments and an orangutan jaw.

The exposure in 1953 removed Piltdown from the human family tree and became a classic example of how expectation, prestige, and insufficient testing can preserve a bad result.

The precise identity of the forger has been investigated extensively, with Charles Dawson often considered the most likely culprit. That historical question is separate from the scientific conclusion: Piltdown Man itself was a deliberate fabrication.
