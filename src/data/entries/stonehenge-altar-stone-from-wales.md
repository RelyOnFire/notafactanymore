Stonehenge contains stones with different geological histories. Several of its smaller bluestones have Welsh origins. The large sandstone slab called the Altar Stone was also assigned a Welsh source, following a suggestion by geologist H. H. Thomas in 1923. A shared regional origin offered a plausible framework for investigating the monument's stones.

The sandstone did not fit that framework comfortably. Mineralogical and chemical work gradually made a Welsh match harder to sustain. In 2023, researchers explicitly argued that the search should extend beyond the Anglo-Welsh Basin.

The next step examined the histories preserved inside individual mineral grains. Zircon, apatite, and rutile carry information about their ages and parent rocks. A mixture of these grains can identify the geological material from which a sandstone was assembled. In August 2024, Anthony Clarke and colleagues reported a combination pointing to the Orcadian Basin in northeast Scotland.

That placed the roughly six-tonne Altar Stone at least 750 kilometres from its geological source region. It did not relocate every Stonehenge stone to Scotland, or erase the evidence for Welsh bluestones. One substantial piece of the monument acquired a different origin.

The result also left a conspicuous gap. Matching a source region does not identify a particular quarry, the people involved, or a route across prehistoric Britain. The researchers discussed transport possibilities, but the mineral measurements cannot by themselves show how the stone travelled. They date geological grains, not its movement to the monument.

The numerical dates record the 1923 proposal and the 2024 result. Because the Welsh attribution was questioned before 2024 and was never an evenly held consensus, this entry is excluded from belief-lifespan comparisons.
