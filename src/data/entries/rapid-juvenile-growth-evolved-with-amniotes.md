Early tetrapods were often reconstructed as slow-growing animals with life histories broadly resembling modern amphibians. Rapid juvenile growth was therefore treated as a later specialization associated with amniotes, the lineage containing reptiles, birds, and mammals.

Bone histology from *Whatcheeria deltae*, a large stem tetrapod that lived about 330 million years ago, challenged that reconstruction. Researchers examined nine femora spanning different sizes and growth stages. The smaller bones contained fibrolamellar tissue, which forms rapidly and is associated with high growth rates.

That fast-growing juvenile tissue became increasingly remodeled as the animals matured. Adult bones therefore preserved much less evidence of the early growth burst, helping explain why mature specimens alone could support the older slow-growth interpretation.

*Whatcheeria* does not show that every early tetrapod grew quickly. Other early forms retained slower strategies. It does show that rapid juvenile growth was already present deep in tetrapod history rather than appearing only with amniotes. Early tetrapods had a wider range of growth strategies than the old ancestral model allowed.
