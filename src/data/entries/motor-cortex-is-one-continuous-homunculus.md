For generations, diagrams of the motor cortex showed a distorted little human stretched along the precentral gyrus. The foot sat near the top, followed by leg, trunk, hand and face. This motor homunculus came from pioneering stimulation studies and became one of neuroscience's most familiar maps.

The broad ordering was never imaginary. Moving the foot, hand or mouth really does activate different parts of primary motor cortex. Researchers had also known for years that representations overlap and that the fine detail is messier than the famous drawing suggests.

In 2023, high-resolution fMRI exposed a larger break in the simple picture. The major foot, hand and mouth regions were separated by areas with different structure and connectivity. Those intervening regions were strongly connected to one another and to systems involved in action planning, arousal, pain and physiological regulation.

The researchers called this system the somato-cognitive action network, or SCAN. The pattern appeared across multiple large datasets and was also supported by developmental and macaque observations.

The motor homunculus still captures a real somatotopic principle. What it no longer captures is the whole architecture of primary motor cortex. Rather than one continuous body map, motor cortex contains alternating regions for relatively specific effectors and a network that helps coordinate actions of the body as a whole.
