Mammalian nasal passages contain delicate scroll-like bones called turbinals. The maxilloturbinals support respiratory tissues that warm and humidify incoming air and recover heat and moisture during exhalation.

That function suggested a useful fossil clue. Animals with high metabolic rates ventilate their lungs heavily, so large respiratory turbinals were proposed as a skeletal signature of endothermy. A prominent 1994 study called maxilloturbinals the first reliable morphological indicator of endothermy available in the fossil record.

The assumption was difficult to test because metabolic rate is not preserved in bone. In 2023, researchers instead asked whether the proposed relationship actually holds across living mammals. They used CT scans from 424 specimens representing 310 species and compared maxilloturbinal surface area with measured physiological traits.

After correcting for body size and evolutionary relationships, maxilloturbinal size did not significantly track either body temperature or basal metabolic rate. Much of the variation was better explained by ecology and phylogeny.

Respiratory turbinals still have important roles in heat and water exchange. What failed was the stronger inference that their size provides a dependable readout of an animal's metabolic level. Fossil maxilloturbinals can inform respiratory anatomy, but they are not a simple endothermy thermometer.
