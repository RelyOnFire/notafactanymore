Few extinct animals look more difficult to keep standing than a giant sauropod. Their bodies could weigh tens of tonnes, their necks and tails stretched for many metres, and nothing alive on land today approaches the largest of them.

For generations, water seemed to solve the engineering problem.

From the late nineteenth century well into the twentieth, sauropods were repeatedly reconstructed in lakes and swamps. If their legs could not carry such enormous bodies for long, buoyancy could. Long necks could reach the surface while much of the animal remained submerged, and the swamp-dwelling image became so familiar that it survived in museums, books and popular art even when contrary evidence already existed.

The idea was never completely unanimous. Some paleontologists argued surprisingly early that sauropods were capable land animals. Elmer Riggs, for example, made the case in the early twentieth century. But those arguments did not immediately dislodge the amphibious giant from the standard picture.

By the 1960s and 1970s, the balance of evidence was changing. Sauropod trackways recorded enormous animals supporting themselves and moving across terrestrial surfaces. Their column-like limbs, feet, vertebral column and weight-bearing anatomy increasingly looked like adaptations to carrying a huge body on land rather than compromises for an aquatic life.

Walter Coombs pulled much of that evidence together in a major 1975 review. He found that where sauropod anatomy allowed a firm ecological interpretation, it generally favored terrestrial behavior. The deep chest itself could be understood as part of the machinery for bearing weight on land.

Later work added an ironic twist. Sauropods possessed extensive air spaces in their skeletons and probably a bird-like system of respiratory air sacs. Computer models incorporating that pneumatic anatomy found that a deeply immersed sauropod would be extremely buoyant. Rather than resting securely on the bottom with water conveniently holding up its mass, the animal would tend to float and could be unstable.

None of this means sauropods avoided water. Like large land animals today, they could wade, cross rivers and probably swim. Fossil trackways may record some such behavior.

What disappeared was the necessity. Sauropods did not require a swamp to make their size physically possible. The giants that once seemed too heavy for land had evolved precisely the anatomy needed to live on it.
