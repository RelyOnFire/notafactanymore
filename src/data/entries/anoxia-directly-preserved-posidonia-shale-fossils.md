The Posidonia Shale of southwestern Germany is famous for exquisitely preserved Jurassic marine life, including articulated skeletons and traces of soft tissue. The deposit formed during an interval of widespread ocean deoxygenation.

For decades it served as a classic example of the stagnation model of exceptional fossil preservation. In that picture, poorly circulated, oxygen-free bottom water limited scavengers and decay, allowing bodies to survive long enough to fossilize.

Anoxia did matter, but the chemistry proved more complicated. A 2023 study examined the mineral composition of roughly 70 fossils and reassessed the geology of the deposit. Many non-biomineralized tissues were preserved through early phosphatization: their remains were rapidly replaced or stabilized by phosphate minerals.

The sequence of minerals indicated that this process occurred under suboxic conditions and during episodes when oxygen returned to the bottom water. Those changes created steep redox boundaries that concentrated microbial and chemical reactions around buried remains.

Persistent anoxia alone can therefore be too simple an explanation and may even impede some routes to exceptional preservation. The newer model keeps oxygen-poor conditions as part of the setting but gives rapid burial, changing redox conditions and early mineralization the decisive role in preserving the Posidonia fossils.
