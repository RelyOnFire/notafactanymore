Canonical oxygenic photosynthesis uses two light-driven reaction centers. Photosystem II extracts electrons from water and releases oxygen; Photosystem I re-energizes those electrons so they can help produce the reducing power needed for carbon fixation. In plants, algae, and ordinary cyanobacteria, removing PSI prevents photoautotrophic growth.

A 2026 experiment showed that this requirement is not absolute. Researchers engineered *Synechocystis* strains lacking functional PSI and then subjected them to adaptive laboratory evolution under very low light. Several lineages acquired mutations that restored slow photoautotrophic growth.

A derivative lacking both native cyanobacterial and introduced plant PSI genes still fixed inorganic carbon and released oxygen in the light. The proposed bypass uses other thylakoid components, including reverse electron flow through NDH-1, to generate reducing power without PSI. The strains remained inefficient and light-sensitive: measured net oxygen evolution was only about 24–29% of wild type.

No naturally occurring PSI-free oxygenic phototroph is known, and the normal pathway still uses both photosystems. The narrowed conclusion is that PSI is not biochemically indispensable under every possible biological configuration. Laboratory evolution found a workable, if inefficient, route around one of photosynthesis's canonical components.
