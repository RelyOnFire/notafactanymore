Sponges can squeeze and contract their bodies even though they have no true muscle cells. That made them important for reconstructing how animal muscles evolved. One simple reading was that muscle appeared after the sponge lineage had split from other animals, leaving sponge contraction as a separate solution.

The cells doing the work were poorly characterized.

In 2022, researchers studied the freshwater sponge *Ephydatia muelleri*. Its contracting canal tissues contained an organized molecular system built from actin, striated-muscle myosin II and transgelin. Contraction depended on calcium signalling through a pathway also used in other contractile tissues. Development of the system was linked to MRTF, a transcriptional regulator involved in muscle development and repair in other animals.

Sponges still do not have true myocytes, and the finding did not reclassify their tissues as muscles. Instead, it pushed part of the history of muscle further back.

The molecular module that later became central to animal muscle was already present in a multifunctional contractile tissue before modern muscle cells evolved.
